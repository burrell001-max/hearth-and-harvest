Knife Skills: Four Cuts That Do Most of the Work

By Kent B WilliamsPublished 26 November 2026Updated 26 November 20264 min read

Most recipes ask for slicing, dicing, shredding or mincing. Learning those four properly is faster than learning twenty, and it makes the cooking that follows much more predictable.

The grip, and why it matters more than speed

Hold the knife with the blade pinched between thumb and forefinger, just in front of the handle, and the remaining three fingers wrapped around it. This feels odd for a week and then feels like the only sensible way to hold a knife, because it puts the blade in line with your forearm and gives you control over the tip.

The other hand does the guiding and never leaves the board. Curl the fingertips under so the knuckles face the blade, and let the flat of the knife rest against them as you cut. The knuckles act as a fence. Speed comes later, and only once the fence is automatic.

Slicing: the cut behind everything else

Slicing is a push or a draw, not a chop. Start with the tip on the board and draw the knife back and down through the food, or start with the heel and push forward. Either way the blade travels along its length, which is what actually separates the food; pressing straight down is how you crush a tomato.

For anything round, take a thin slice off one side first so it sits flat. A pepper halved through the stem, an onion halved root to tip, a potato laid on its flat face: all of them are safer and easier to cut once they are not rolling.

Practise on an onion, which is cheap and forgiving. Halve it root to tip, lay the flat side down, make horizontal cuts towards the root, then vertical cuts, then cross-cuts. The root holds the layers together while you work, and you trim it off at the end. Doing this slowly twenty times teaches more than any video.

Dicing: cutting to a size, not to a shape

A dice is a slice, then a stack, then a cross-cut. Slice the food into planks of the thickness you want the dice to be, stack a few planks, cut them into batons, then turn the batons and cut across. The pieces come out even because each step is measured against the one before it.

Evenness matters more than neatness. An onion diced into a mixture of fine and chunky pieces will burn at the fine end before the chunky end is soft, and no amount of stirring fixes that. If a recipe says half-inch dice, the useful part of the instruction is the half inch.

Shredding and mincing

Shredding leafy things means stacking the leaves, rolling them tightly into a cigar, and slicing across the roll as thinly as you can. This is how basil and mint are cut for a finished dish, and it is gentler on the leaves than chopping, which bruises them and turns them dark.

Mincing is a rocking motion: the tip stays on the board, the heel comes up and down, and the knife pivots through the pile, turning it as you go. Garlic, ginger and herbs are all minced this way. Sprinkling a pinch of salt over garlic as you mince stops it sliding around and helps it break down into a paste.

Sharp beats fast, every time

A dull knife is the dangerous one. It skids off tomato skin and onion rounds, and it needs so much pressure that when it does bite, it goes further than you intended. A sharp knife starts the cut where you put it, which is most of what safety in a kitchen comes down to.

Hone the edge before you cook, with the steel that came with the knife or a ceramic rod, holding it at roughly the angle of the existing bevel and drawing the blade down and across. Honing straightens the edge rather than sharpening it. A proper sharpen once or twice a year, by stone or by a shop, does the rest.

Never put a good knife in the dishwasher. The heat and the long soak dull the edge and can loosen the handle, and the blade knocking against other cutlery does the rest. Wash it by hand, dry it immediately, and put it away dry.

A board setup that makes it easy

Put a damp cloth under the board so it does not move, and give yourself a bowl for scraps and a bowl for the cut food. That is the entire setup. Cutting over a bin, or sweeping peelings onto the floor for later, is how a five-minute job becomes a fifteen-minute one.

Wood or plastic both work, and both should be washed properly after raw meat. Keeping one board for meat and one for everything else removes the question entirely, which is easier than remembering which way round it was last time.

Storing a knife so it stays sharp

A magnetic strip on the wall, a knife block, or the original box all work. What does not work is a drawer full of loose cutlery, where the edge knocks against metal every time the drawer opens. If a drawer is the only option, keep the knife in a sheath or slide a folded tea towel around it.

Keep the blade dry between uses. Water left on a carbon steel blade will spot it within an hour, and on any knife it dulls the edge faster than cutting does. A quick wipe after washing is the whole habit.
