How to Season, Clean and Keep a Cast Iron Skillet

By Kent B WilliamsPublished 26 November 2026Updated 26 November 20264 min read

Cast iron is not fragile, but it does have rules. Most of the pans that get thrown out were damaged by soap, soaking or a long simmer, all of which can be avoided.

What seasoning actually is

Seasoning is not a coating applied once. It is a thin layer of oil that has been heated past its smoke point and polymerised into a hard, smooth, slightly glossy film bonded to the iron. It darkens with use and builds up in layers, which is why an old pan behaves differently from a new one.

Because it is a chemical layer rather than a paint, it can be removed by anything that dissolves it or cooks it off: long contact with water, acidic food simmered for an hour, or dish soap used aggressively. The pan is not ruined when that happens. It simply needs the layer rebuilt.

First use of a new pan

Most new cast iron arrives pre-seasoned, with a factory coating that is thin but functional. Wash off the shipping oil with hot water and a little soap, dry it thoroughly on the hob, then rub a very thin film of neutral oil over the whole pan, inside and out, and heat it until it just begins to smoke. Wipe out any excess and let it cool.

The mistake at this stage is using too much oil. A thick coat turns sticky rather than hard, and a sticky pan is worse than a bare one because everything sticks to it. Thin is the whole idea: wipe the oil on, then wipe it off as though you have changed your mind.

Everyday cleaning

While the pan is still warm, rinse it under hot water and scrub with a brush or a chain mail scrubber. A little soap is fine on a well-seasoned pan; the old advice against it dates from when soap contained lye, which would strip the seasoning. What does damage is soaking, so do not leave it in the sink.

Dry it immediately, on the hob over low heat, until every trace of water has gone. Water left in the pan is what causes rust, and rust is the only common problem that needs real work to fix. Once dry, wipe a few drops of oil around the inside with a paper towel and put it away.

If the pan came from a market or an inherited kitchen, check it before cooking. A wobbly base will rock on a hob and cook unevenly, and a crack across the base cannot be repaired. Surface rust and thick black build-up, on the other hand, are both fixable, and neither is a reason to leave the pan behind.

When it needs reseasoning

If food starts sticking in the middle of the pan, or the surface turns patchy grey, the seasoning has worn thin. Clean and dry the pan, rub it with a thin coat of oil, and bake it upside down at 450°F (230°C) for an hour with a sheet of foil on the rack below to catch drips. Let it cool in the oven.

Two or three rounds of that will bring back a pan that has been scrubbed too hard. A pan that has been badly neglected, with thick sticky patches, can be stripped and started again, but it is rarely necessary. Most pans described as ruined just need oil and an hour.

Rust, and how to deal with it

Surface rust is orange and comes off with a scouring pad and hot water. Scrub it back to bare grey iron, dry the pan on the hob, and reseason. Rust that has pitted the surface deeply is a different matter, but it takes years of neglect to get there.

The prevention is dull and effective: dry the pan on the heat every time, never stack it wet, and keep a paper towel between pans if they are stored in a pile. In a humid kitchen, a quick wipe of oil after each use is the difference between a pan that lasts decades and one that does not.

A rusted pan is not a lost cause, though it looks like one. Scrub it back with steel wool and hot water until you reach grey iron, dry it on the hob, and run two rounds of oiling and baking. It will come back darker and more even than it went.

What to cook, and what to avoid, in a new pan

A young pan benefits from fat and heat: cornbread, roast chicken, fried eggs, sausages, and anything shallow-fried. These build the layer while asking little of it. Cornbread in particular is a good first bake, because the pan gets properly hot and the batter leaves a film of fat behind.

Avoid simmering tomatoes, wine reductions and long braises in a pan that is still building its seasoning; the acid will strip it. Once the pan is dark and glassy, an hour of tomato sauce will do it no harm. Cast iron also heats unevenly compared with a modern pan, so give it five minutes to come up to temperature before the food goes in.

Choosing a size that gets used

A ten-inch skillet is the one that earns its space: big enough for four chicken thighs or a whole cornbread, small enough to heat evenly on a domestic hob. A twelve-inch pan is useful for a family roast but heavy to lift when full, and a six-inch pan is for eggs and little else.

Weight matters more than people expect. A modern cast iron pan is often lighter than a vintage one, and for daily cooking the lighter pan is the one you will actually reach for. Hold it in the shop before buying, ideally with a bag of flour in it.
