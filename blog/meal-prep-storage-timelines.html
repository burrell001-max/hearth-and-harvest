How Long Cooked Food Really Keeps in the Fridge

By Kent B WilliamsPublished 26 November 2026Updated 26 November 20264 min read

Most food poisoning at home comes from time and temperature rather than from anything visible. A few rules cover nearly all of it.

The two numbers that matter

Food should not sit between roughly 40°F and 140°F (4°C and 60°C) for more than two hours in total. That range is where bacteria multiply fastest, and the clock runs during cooling as well as during the meal. A pot of stew left on the hob overnight has spent hours in exactly the wrong zone.

The second number is your fridge temperature. Most fridges run warmer than people think, and a fridge at 45°F (7°C) will not keep food as long as one at 38°F (3°C). A cheap fridge thermometer is the single most useful thing you can put in there.

Cooling is the step people skip

A large pot of soup will sit in the warm zone for hours if it goes into the fridge whole. Divide it into shallow containers first, or stand the pot in a sink of cold water and stir occasionally until it stops steaming. The aim is to get it under 40°F (4°C) within a couple of hours.

Do not put hot food straight into a sealed container and into the fridge either. It warms everything around it and condenses water on the lid, which then drips back into the food. Leave the lid off until it has stopped steaming, then seal it.

Rough timelines by category

Cooked meat, poultry and fish keep three to four days in the fridge. Soups, stews and chili keep three to four days, and taste better on the second. Cooked rice and grains are the ones to be careful with: cool them quickly, refrigerate within an hour, and use within a day, because cooked rice supports a bacterium that survives reheating.

Cooked vegetables and roasted trays keep three to four days, though they lose texture after two. Cut fruit keeps three days; whole fruit keeps much longer. Egg dishes, quiche and frittata are best within three days. Anything containing cream or raw egg should be treated as a two-day item.

These are guides rather than guarantees, and the honest variable is how the food was handled before it went in. Something cooked, cooled quickly and refrigerated straight away will comfortably reach the top of its range. The same dish left on the hob for an hour before it was boxed is a day further along.

What freezing changes

Freezing stops the clock rather than resetting it. Cooked stews, soups and braises freeze well for two to three months; cooked pasta and rice freeze acceptably but go soft. Dairy sauces split on thawing, and vegetables with a high water content turn mushy, which is why a soup with potatoes in it is better frozen before the potatoes go in.

Label everything with a date and what it is. A frozen block of brown liquid is indistinguishable from another frozen block of brown liquid, and unlabelled food is food you will not use. Freeze flat in bags for fast thawing, and thaw in the fridge rather than on the counter.

Some things do not freeze well no matter what the label says. Potatoes turn grainy, cucumber and lettuce collapse, and sauces thickened with cornflour can separate on thawing. Cooked rice freezes better than most people expect if it is spread flat and cooled fast.

Reading the signs honestly

Smell is a poor guide on its own: the bacteria that make food dangerous do not usually change how it smells. If you are unsure how long something has been in there, the answer is to throw it out, and the honest fix is to label containers so the question does not come up.

Visible mould means the whole container goes, not just the mouldy part, since the roots run deeper than the surface. A film or bubbles on a sauce that was not there before is also a reason to discard. Reheating kills some bacteria but not the toxins they have already produced, so reheating spoiled food does not make it safe.

Habits that remove the guesswork

Keep one shelf for leftovers and put new containers at the back, so the older ones come forward. Write the date on the lid with a marker, not the bottom of the container where you cannot see it. Do a five-minute sweep before each shop and plan the next two dinners around what needs eating.

None of this needs special equipment beyond containers that stack and a thermometer. The point is not to be fussy; it is to make the fridge legible, so you can see what you have, use it in time, and stop paying for food twice.

Containers, and why shape matters

Shallow containers cool faster and stack better in the fridge, which means you can see what you have. Glass is easier to clean and does not hold onto smells, but it is heavier and it does not travel well. For the freezer, bags that can be flattened are more useful than boxes, because a flat bag thaws in a fraction of the time.

Keep one size that nests and buy several of them. Mismatched containers are why leftovers never get used: nothing stacks, the shelf becomes a jumble, and the container at the back is forgotten until it announces itself.
