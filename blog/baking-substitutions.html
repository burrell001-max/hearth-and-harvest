Baking Substitutions That Work (and the Ones That Don't)

By Kent B WilliamsPublished 26 November 2026Updated 26 November 20264 min read

Cooking forgives substitutions because you can taste as you go. Baking is chemistry you cannot correct afterwards, so it pays to know which swaps are safe and which are a different recipe.

Why baking is less forgiving

In a savoury dish, an ingredient swap changes flavour. In a bake, it also changes structure, and structure is the part you cannot fix once the tin is in the oven. Flour provides gluten, eggs set the crumb, sugar holds water and browns, and fat shortens the strands. Change one and the others have to compensate.

That does not mean substitutions are off limits. It means the safe ones are the ones that preserve the job the ingredient was doing, not the ones that look similar in the cupboard. Buttermilk is acid; milk is not. Butter is fat plus water; oil is fat alone. Those differences matter.

There is one substitution that improves almost every bake and costs nothing: weighing instead of scooping. A cup of flour can vary by twenty per cent depending on how it was packed, and that difference is enough to turn a tender cake into a dry one. A cheap digital scale removes the guesswork permanently.

Buttermilk, and the swaps that genuinely work

Buttermilk is there for acidity, which reacts with baking soda to make a lift, and for a slight tang. Stir a tablespoon of lemon juice or white vinegar into a cup of milk and leave it for ten minutes and you get an acceptable stand-in for most cakes, pancakes and waffles.

Plain yogurt thinned with milk works too, and is closer in texture. What does not work is sour cream at full strength, which is too thick and too rich, or milk alone, which leaves the bake flat and slightly soapy if the recipe relies on soda for its rise.

Fats: butter, oil and everything between

Butter brings flavour and, in cookies and pastry, structure from the way it creeps when cold. Oil brings moisture and tenderness but no flavour of its own. Replacing butter with oil in a muffin or a loaf cake usually works at about three-quarters of the volume; replacing it in a cookie or a pie crust does not, because the dough behaves completely differently.

Melted butter is not the same as softened butter either. Melting breaks the emulsion, so a cookie made with melted butter spreads more and bakes flatter. If a recipe says softened, it means softened, and the microwave is a poor tool for getting there.

Eggs, sugar and the leaveners

Eggs are structure, moisture and lift, and they are hard to replace without changing the bake. A flax or chia egg holds things together in a brownie but will not whip into a meringue. If a recipe uses three eggs, one substitute is usually safe; three is a different recipe.

Sugar does more than sweeten: it keeps the crumb soft and helps browning. Cutting it by a quarter is generally fine. Cutting it in half makes a drier, paler bake that stales faster. Baking powder and soda are not interchangeable either; soda needs an acid to work, powder brings its own, and swapping them unbalances the whole formula.

Flour, and the one swap that usually holds

All-purpose flour can be replaced by bread flour in most cakes and cookies without disaster, though the result will be slightly chewier. Cake flour makes a lighter crumb and needs more of it by volume. Whole wheat flour absorbs more water, so it needs extra liquid and produces a denser bake.

The swap that reliably fails is substituting a gluten-free blend one-for-one without checking whether it already contains a binder. Some do, some do not, and adding xanthan gum to a blend that already has it produces a gummy result that no amount of baking can fix.

The honest rule

If you are missing a minor ingredient, swap it. If you are missing a structural one, change the plan: make something else, or go to the shop. A cake made with half the butter and no eggs is not a cake with substitutions, it is a different and worse thing, and the ingredients are too expensive to waste finding that out.

Keep a short list of swaps you have actually tested and know work in your oven. Two or three reliable ones are worth more than a long list of maybes, and they stop the moment of panic in the middle of a recipe turning into a bake that goes in the bin.

If a swap is untested, make a half batch the first time. It costs less and tells you whether the texture survived, which is the thing a substitution most often changes and the thing you cannot tell from the raw batter.

Measuring, the variable behind most failures

Scooping flour straight from the bag compacts it, and the amount in the cup can be a third more than the recipe intended. Spooning it into the cup and levelling the top is better; weighing is better still, and most modern recipes list grams for exactly this reason.

Oven temperature is the other hidden variable. An oven that runs twenty degrees cool will leave a cake pale and sunken while the recipe's timings insist it is done. An inexpensive oven thermometer clipped to the shelf will tell you more about your baking than any new tin.
