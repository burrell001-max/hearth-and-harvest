Skillet Cornbread

Prep Time: 10 mins Cook Time: 22 mins Servings: 8 Category: Dinner

Illustrative image.

Cornbread baked in a hot cast iron skillet has a crust that a baking dish cannot produce. The batter hits the oiled metal and starts setting on contact, so the outside fries to a deep gold while the middle stays tender and slightly damp.

The rest is a matter of taste. Northern versions are a little sweet and cake-like, southern ones are savoury and coarser. This sits in the middle: a few tablespoons of sugar for flavour, but not enough to make it a dessert.

Ingredients

  • 3 tbsp bacon fat or vegetable oil
  • 1 1/4 cups medium-grind cornmeal
  • 1 cup all-purpose flour
  • 3 tbsp granulated sugar
  • 2 tsp baking powder
  • 1/2 tsp baking soda
  • 1 1/4 tsp kosher salt
  • 1 1/2 cups buttermilk
  • 2 large eggs
  • 4 tbsp unsalted butter, melted

Instructions

  1. Put a 10-inch cast iron skillet with the bacon fat into the oven and heat to 425°F (220°C). The pan needs a full 15 minutes to get properly hot.
  2. Whisk the cornmeal, flour, sugar, baking powder, baking soda and salt in a large bowl.
  3. In a second bowl, whisk the buttermilk, eggs and melted butter until smooth, then pour the wet ingredients into the dry and stir just until combined. A few lumps are better than a smooth batter; overmixing makes the crumb tough.
  4. Pull the hot skillet out, swirl the fat so it coats the sides, and pour the excess into the batter, stirring it in quickly. Pour the batter back into the smoking-hot pan; it should sizzle at the edges immediately.
  5. Bake for 20 to 22 minutes, until the top is golden and a skewer in the centre comes out clean. Cool in the pan for 10 minutes, then cut into wedges.

Why this recipe works

Preheating the pan with the fat in it does two things: it creates the sizzle that sets the crust, and it means the batter starts baking the moment it is poured, giving a faster, more even rise. The fat poured into the batter carries flavour through the crumb. Stirring only until the flour disappears keeps the gluten from developing, which is why this stays tender rather than bready.

Tips for success

  • Heat the skillet for a full 15 minutes. A pan that has only just reached temperature will not sizzle.
  • Let the batter rest for five minutes before pouring. The cornmeal hydrates and the crumb comes out less gritty.
  • Pour the fat from the hot pan into the batter, stir, then pour back. It is faster and safer than trying to grease the pan twice.
  • Do not open the oven before 18 minutes. Cornbread falls if the heat drops early.
  • Cool in the pan for ten minutes, then turn out, so the base stays crisp rather than sweating.

Substitutions that work

  • No buttermilk? Stir a tablespoon of lemon juice into a cup and a half of milk and leave it for ten minutes.
  • Medium-grind cornmeal gives the best texture; coarse polenta grind needs a longer rest and more liquid.
  • Bacon fat produces the crispest edge, but vegetable oil or melted butter both work.
  • For a savoury version, leave out the sugar and add a cup of grated cheddar and a chopped jalapeno.

Storage and make-ahead

Keep covered at room temperature for two days, or in the fridge for four. Reheat in a 350°F (175°C) oven for eight minutes, which restores the crust far better than a microwave. Cut squares freeze for three months and thaw on the counter.

Serving suggestions

Serve warm with butter and honey, or alongside chili, roast chicken or a bowl of beans. Day-old cornbread is excellent cubed, tossed with oil and toasted for croutons, or crumbled into stuffing.

Frequently asked questions

Can I make this without buttermilk?

Yes. Add a tablespoon of lemon juice or white vinegar to milk and let it stand for ten minutes, or thin plain yogurt with milk until it pours.

Can I bake it in a regular dish?

Yes, but preheat the dish and the fat in the oven first, and expect a softer crust and a few extra minutes of baking time.

Is cornbread supposed to be sweet?

That depends entirely on where you grew up. Three tablespoons of sugar is a middle ground; leave it out for a southern-style bread, or double it for a northern one.
