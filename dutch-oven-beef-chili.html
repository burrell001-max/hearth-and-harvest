Dutch Oven Beef Chili

Prep Time: 20 mins Cook Time: 1 hr 30 mins Servings: 6 Category: Dinner

Illustrative image.

Chili is easy to make and surprisingly easy to make badly. The usual failure is a pot that tastes thin and sour, with grey meat and a spice flavour that sits on top rather than in the sauce. The fix is not a secret ingredient so much as an order of operations: brown the beef properly, cook the spices in fat, and give the pot an hour to come together.

A heavy Dutch oven does the first twenty minutes on the hob, then holds enough heat to keep a bare simmer going on the lowest setting.

Ingredients

  • 2 lb ground beef, 80/20
  • 1 large yellow onion, diced
  • 1 green bell pepper, diced
  • 4 cloves garlic, minced
  • 2 tbsp chili powder
  • 1 tbsp ground cumin
  • 1 tsp smoked paprika
  • 1/2 tsp cayenne pepper, or to taste
  • 2 tbsp tomato paste
  • 1 can (28 oz) crushed tomatoes
  • 2 cans (15 oz each) kidney beans, drained and rinsed
  • 1 1/2 cups beef stock
  • 1 tbsp unsweetened cocoa powder
  • 1 tsp kosher salt
  • 1 tbsp apple cider vinegar
  • Sour cream, shredded cheddar and sliced scallions, to serve

Instructions

  1. Set a heavy Dutch oven over medium-high heat. Add the beef in two batches and cook, breaking it up with a wooden spoon, until the water has cooked off and the edges of the meat are browned and crisp, about 8 minutes per batch. Lift the beef out and leave the fat behind.
  2. Lower the heat to medium. Add the onion and bell pepper and cook in the beef fat for 6 to 7 minutes until soft and lightly browned. Add the garlic and stir for 45 seconds, just until it smells sweet.
  3. Stir in the chili powder, cumin, paprika, cayenne and tomato paste. Cook the spices in the fat for a full minute, stirring constantly, which is what turns them from dusty into deep and rounded.
  4. Return the beef to the pot with the crushed tomatoes, beans, stock, cocoa and salt. Bring to a bare simmer, then cover partially and cook on low for 1 hour, stirring every 15 minutes and scraping the bottom so nothing catches.
  5. Uncover and simmer for a final 15 to 20 minutes until the chili is thick enough that a spoon dragged across the bottom leaves a trail that fills in slowly. Stir in the vinegar, taste, and adjust the salt before serving with the toppings.

Why this recipe works

Three things carry the flavour. Browning the beef in batches builds a crust that a single crowded batch would steam away, and those browned bits dissolve into the sauce later. Blooming the spices in the rendered fat for a full minute cooks out the raw, dusty edge that pre-ground chili powder carries. The cocoa adds bitterness and body that rounds off the acid of the tomatoes. The vinegar at the end is the last adjustment, brightening everything the long simmer flattened.

Tips for success

  • Brown in two batches and do not stir much. Constant stirring keeps the meat from ever touching the pan long enough to colour.
  • Fry the tomato paste with the spices until it darkens from bright red to brick, which takes about a minute.
  • Keep the simmer low enough that a bubble breaks the surface every second or two. A rolling boil makes the meat stringy.
  • Let it rest off the heat for 15 minutes before serving. It thickens as it stands and the flavours settle.

Substitutions that work

  • Ground turkey or chicken works, but add two extra tablespoons of oil when browning, since leaner meat will not render enough fat.
  • Black beans, pinto beans or a mixture are all fine; cannellini beans make it milder and creamier.
  • Cocoa powder can be replaced with a tablespoon of dark chocolate or left out entirely, though the chili will taste slightly sharper.
  • For less heat, drop the cayenne and use a mild chili powder, then pass hot sauce at the table.

Storage and make-ahead

Chili improves overnight. Keep it covered in the fridge for up to five days, and reheat gently on the hob with a splash of stock, since it thickens considerably when cold. Freeze in portions for up to three months; flat freezer bags thaw fastest.

Serving suggestions

Sour cream, sharp cheddar, sliced scallions and pickled jalapenos, with cornbread or plain rice alongside. It also works over baked potatoes, spooned into soft tortillas, or thinned with stock into a sauce for pasta.

Frequently asked questions

How spicy is this chili?

Moderately, with the amounts written. The cayenne is the only truly hot element, so halving it gives a mild chili that still tastes seasoned, and doubling it makes a genuinely hot one.

Can I make it in a slow cooker instead?

Yes, but brown the beef, soften the vegetables and bloom the spices in a pan first, then transfer everything and cook on low for six hours. Skipping that step is what makes slow cooker chili taste flat.

Do I have to add beans?

No. Leave them out and reduce the stock by half a cup, then simmer a little longer to thicken. The result is closer to a Texas-style bowl of red.
